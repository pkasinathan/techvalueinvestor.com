Higher Rates Are Starting to Bite

September 6, 2026

McDonald’s trades near $300 per share with a modest 2% dividend yield, while the benchmark 10-year U.S. Treasury note offers a risk-free return of 4.77%.

When risk-free government paper yields nearly 5%, investors are forced to reconsider how much they are willing to pay for mature businesses growing in the mid-single digits. The price of capital is re-asserting its authority across consumer staples, corporate credit, and enterprise technology valuations.

Slow Growth Faces Demanding Starting Valuations

McDonald’s currently pays an annual dividend of $7.44 per share, resulting in a dividend payout ratio of 100%. While the business continues to grow revenues at 5% and expands earnings per share at 6% with the assistance of share repurchases, faster growth remains unlikely for a mature global franchise.

In a zero-interest-rate environment, accepting a 2% dividend yield on a blue-chip consumer staple was standard practice. Today, with the 10-year Treasury yielding 4.77%, holding an equity yielding only 2% demands a substantial margin of safety that current share prices do not provide.

Discounted cash flow scenarios illustrate the valuation disconnect. Assuming 5% long-term dividend growth and a 25 terminal multiple produces an implied intrinsic value well below $200 per share. Applying a more conservative value-investing multiple of 15 lands present value closer to $115 to $120. As Peter Lynch warned, investors should avoid slow-growing businesses unless they are priced at clear bargain levels. At $300, McDonald’s functions more as a vulnerable interest rate play than a mispriced opportunity.

Corporate Debt Markets Re-Price Credit Risk

The impact of elevated interest rates is reverberating beyond consumer defensives into leveraged corporate debt markets. Borrowing costs across the riskiest tiers of corporate America have surged to their highest levels since the introduction of sweeping tariff measures, with recent Treasury sell-offs spilling directly into high-yield spreads.

Highly indebted corporations face an unforgiving refinancing calendar. While defensive consumer franchises face multiple compression from income-seeking shareholders, speculative borrowers are confronting punishing coupon rates from institutional lenders. Both pressures trace back to the same fundamental source: the global repricing of sovereign credit.

Strategic Discipline Across Enterprise Technology

Capital allocation discipline is equally vital within the enterprise technology sector. Software investors are adopting rigorous downside tests, focusing on whether emerging operational disruptions threaten total capital loss or merely dampen expected returns.

At Apple, leadership continuity remains central as former Chief Executive Tim Cook maintains strategic advisory oversight under a newly structured $2 million compensation arrangement. Across the market, whether analyzing consumer franchises, high-yield debt, or software platforms, the defining lesson remains constant: when the cost of money rises, starting valuation is the primary determinant of long-term investment survival.
