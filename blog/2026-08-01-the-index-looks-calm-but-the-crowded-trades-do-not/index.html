The Index Looks Calm, but the Crowded Trades Do Not

August 1, 2026 · 3 min read

How can the S&P 500 fall less than 1% while semiconductors lose 20% and a leveraged hedge fund reportedly loses 67%?

The Take

  • Value stocks were up 20%.
  • SpaceX exceeded a $3 trillion market value during its first few trading days and traded above 150 times revenue.
  • GMO forecast an average 8.1% annual real loss for US stocks over seven years, equivalent to a 47% decline.

How can the S&P 500 fall less than 1% while semiconductors lose 20% and a leveraged hedge fund reportedly loses 67%?

The index looks calm, but beneath it, market leadership is changing, leverage is breaking, and popular long-duration bets are demanding far more capital.

The Index Is Hiding the Damage

Value stocks were up 20%. Small caps gained 19%. Emerging markets and mid caps each rose 15%, international stocks gained 13%, and US stocks rose 9%.

Growth stocks declined slightly, while the Magnificent Seven fell 3%. After roughly 15 years of domestic growth and technology leadership, that is a meaningful reversal.

The case for diversification is not that it identifies tomorrow’s winner. It keeps a change in leadership from threatening the entire portfolio.

Semiconductors show how much an index can conceal. Semiconductor stocks gained 237% over 14 months through the end of June, then fell 20% in July while the S&P 500 declined less than 1%.

The DRAM ETF, concentrated primarily in three names, raised almost $30 billion near the peak and then fell 30%. The Situational Awareness hedge fund reportedly declined 67% in July after a margin call.

These losses do not establish that a broad market crash is imminent. They suggest that risk had accumulated inside a small number of crowded and leveraged bets. Once those trades reversed, leverage turned falling prices into forced selling.

A market can punish excess without producing dramatic movement in the index.

Long-Duration Ambitions Need More Capital

SpaceX exceeded a $3 trillion market value during its first few trading days and traded above 150 times revenue. It then fell more than 50%, dropping below both its IPO price and its first-day close.

US IPOs had raised a record $144 billion year to date, with most of that capital coming from SpaceX. A record amount of capital raised does not necessarily indicate broad market strength when one company accounts for most of it.

Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and Meta spent a combined $165 billion on capital expenditures during the quarter, 87% more than a year earlier and 393% more than three years earlier.

Google reported negative free cash flow for the first time in its history. Meta’s free cash flow fell 91% to $784 million.

None of this proves that AI investment is uneconomic. Those expenditures may still generate attractive returns. But the burden of proof is rising as these companies become more capital intensive and financing conditions remain difficult.

Core PCE has stayed above 2% for 64 consecutive months. The 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.2%, its highest level since July 2007, despite 175 basis points of Federal Reserve cuts beginning in 2024.

Real GDP growth declined from 2.8% in 2024 to 2.1% in 2025 and 1.5% in the latest reading. National debt approached $40 trillion.

Markets are funding enormous long-duration ambitions while inflation, long-term rates, federal borrowing, and slower real GDP growth make capital discipline more valuable. The question is whether the eventual returns will justify the capital committed, particularly when free cash flow is already under pressure.

Survival Beats Precision

GMO forecast an average 8.1% annual real loss for US stocks over seven years, equivalent to a 47% decline. Another estimate argued that a return to a mean P/E ratio of 16 would imply a 60% market crash.

Those forecasts express real valuation concerns, but they provide no reliable timing. Following Jeremy Grantham’s bearishness could also have meant missing the bull market since 2022.

A valuation concern can be valid while the timing decision based on it is disastrous. The more useful question is whether an investor could survive financially and remain committed if valuations mean revert.

One proposed defense is global diversification and a value pillar, including companies with a P/E ratio around 10 and growth of 10% to 15%. The precise allocation matters less than the principle.

Investors do not need to predict the next crash. They need to avoid making one crowded theme responsible for a long-term financial goal.
